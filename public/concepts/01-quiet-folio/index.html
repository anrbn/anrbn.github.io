The shape of a good note.

How small observations become useful ideas without turning a notebook into a warehouse.

A good note is not a perfect record. It is a small surface that gives an idea somewhere to land, and enough context that you can recognize it when you return.

Most systems begin with the promise that nothing will be lost. Soon they contain everything: quotations without questions, links without reasons, and careful categories that describe where an idea came from but not why it mattered.

The useful question is simpler: what must this note preserve so that a future version of me can continue the thought?

A note earns its place by reopening a thought, not merely proving that it once occurred.

Small enough to move

Notes become useful when they can sit beside other notes. One observation can be compared with another, contradicted by a third, or borrowed by a project that did not exist when the sentence was written.

This is why the best notes often feel slightly unfinished. They have edges. They invite another line.

Context without ceremony

A date, a source, and one sentence about why you cared are usually enough. The rest can remain light. Good systems lower the cost of returning; great ones make return feel like recognition.
